=== CAPTION ===
91 student essays. Written by AI: 0. Flagged as AI by at least one detector: 89.

That's a Stanford study (2023): 7 AI detectors, essays by students writing English as a second language. Each detector, on average, flagged 61% of them as AI. Essays by US-born students: almost none flagged. The researchers point to how detectors work: they hunt for plain, predictable words, and careful, simple English looks predictable too.

Even the makers warn:
- OpenAI shut its own AI-text classifier in July 2023 for its "low rate of accuracy" (it called human text AI 9% of the time).
- Turnitin's guidance: its AI writing score shouldn't be the sole basis for action against a student.

So keep your drafts and version history from day one (Google Docs and Word keep it for you). If you're flagged, show them and offer to explain your essay out loud. Teachers: a score is a reason to talk, not proof.

=== PINNED_COMMENT ===
Sources: Stanford HAI, "AI-Detectors Biased Against Non-Native English Writers" (May 15, 2023; paper "GPT Detectors are Biased Against Non-native English Writers", arXiv 2304.02819, James Zou and co-authors): 7 detectors, 91 TOEFL essays, 61.22% classified as AI-generated, 89 of 91 flagged by at least one detector, "near-perfect" on US-born eighth-graders' essays. OpenAI's note on its AI classifier ("no longer available" from July 20, 2023, "due to its low rate of accuracy"; reported by Search Engine Land and Business Today). Turnitin's AI writing guidance (quoted by universities, e.g. University of San Diego).
Our own small test (not in the video): two free detectors from Hugging Face on 4 texts written before chat AIs and 3 real AI texts. OpenAI's 2019 detector (built for GPT-2-era text) called the 1776 US Declaration of Independence AI (55%) and called 2 of the 3 AI texts human; a 2023 detector got all 7 right. 7 short texts prove nothing either way, which is the point: one score is not proof.
