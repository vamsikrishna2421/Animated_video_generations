Same salary. Same loan. Never missed an EMI. An AI loan check said "low risk" 5 times in 10 for one message, and never for the other.

We made up one applicant: ₹45,000 a month, a store supervisor for 4 years, a ₹6,000 bike EMI never missed, asking ₹2 lakh over 24 months for a sister's wedding. We wrote the same facts different ways and asked a small free AI on our own computer to pre-screen the loan, 10 fresh chats each. "Low risk" out of 10:
- a polished letter: 5
- the same message typed like a phone chat: 0
- Telugu in English letters: 0 (once it said he had "a history of missing payments"; he never missed one)
- a plain list of the facts: 0
- told to ignore grammar and language: 1
- the AI's own polished rewrite: 1
- two more formal letters we wrote: 2 and 0
Every other answer was "medium", never "high". Whether low or medium is right isn't the point: the same facts should get the same answer, and here the words moved it. (At a real lender, a worse risk label can mean more questions, a smaller amount, a higher rate, or a no.)

Not just small models: a 2024 study in Nature (Hofmann et al.) found language models judged people more harshly for the same content written in African American English, a US dialect: lower-prestige jobs, and more "guilty" verdicts in made-up court cases (69% vs 62%), though race was never mentioned.

If a bank turns down your loan, ask for the reason in writing. RBI's rules for commercial banks say the bank shall give the main reasons in writing, for all loans, credit cards too. (That rule is written for banks. With a loan app, ask which bank or NBFC is actually lending, and ask them.)

Try it: write the same request twice, once as a formal letter and once the way you'd text it. Ask an AI to rate both. Same facts, same answer?
Building a screener? Score the facts, not the wording: a plain list of the facts got the same answer 10 times out of 10. Test it with the same facts worded differently before it sees real people.
